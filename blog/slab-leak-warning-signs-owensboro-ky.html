How to Tell If You Have a Slab Leak in Owensboro, KY

Nine warning signs, safe checks you can make at home, and the next step when water may be escaping beneath a concrete slab.

A slab leak is a water-supply or drain-line leak located beneath or within a concrete foundation. Because the pipe is hidden, the first clues are often indirect: a meter that keeps moving, an unexplained change in water use, a warm patch on the floor, or a sound that seems to come from below the house. One clue does not confirm a slab leak, but several together deserve prompt investigation.

Quick answer: If every faucet and water-using appliance is off but the meter still shows flow, a leak may be present. That test cannot tell you whether the leak is under the slab, inside a wall, in a fixture, or outdoors, so use it as a reason to investigate—not as a diagnosis.

9 Warning Signs of a Possible Slab Leak

These signs can have other explanations, but they are useful clues when they appear together or continue without an obvious cause.

1. An unexplained water-bill increase

Compare recent use with a similar month when household habits were normal. A sustained change without a new appliance, irrigation use, or occupancy change is worth checking.

2. A meter that moves with water off

After shutting down indoor and outdoor water use, watch the meter or low-flow indicator. Movement suggests flow somewhere in the plumbing system.

3. The sound of running or hissing water

A quiet house can make a pressurized leak easier to hear. Listen near floors and utility areas, but remember that sound can travel through pipes and framing.

4. A warm or damp floor area

A hot-water-line leak may create a warm patch. Moisture can show as damp carpet, warped flooring, or water appearing near a baseboard.

5. Lower water pressure at several fixtures

A supply-line leak can affect pressure across the home. Check whether the change is widespread rather than limited to one aerator or fixture.

6. Musty smells or floor-level mold

Persistent moisture beneath flooring or near baseboards can contribute to odors or visible growth. Do not disturb suspect materials if you are sensitive to mold.

7. Cracks or changes around floors and walls

New cracks, lifting flooring, or doors that begin sticking can have many causes. When paired with moisture or water-flow clues, they warrant a professional look.

8. Water collecting near the foundation

Unexpected wet areas outside may come from grading, irrigation, a service line, or a plumbing leak. Note when and where the water appears before calling.

9. A recurring problem after surface repairs

If a floor, wall, or fixture has been repaired but moisture or water use changes return, the source may be hidden elsewhere in the system.

A Safe Homeowner Check: Watch the Water Meter

A meter check is a useful first observation when you suspect a hidden leak. Turn off faucets, showers, washing machines, dishwashers, ice makers, irrigation, and any other water use. Wait a few minutes, then watch the meter or its leak-flow indicator. If it moves, water is flowing somewhere. Repeat the test if needed and write down the time, meter reading, and any unusual sounds.

The result does not identify the leak’s location. Toilets, outdoor lines, water softeners, irrigation systems, and appliance connections can all produce flow. If the movement continues and you cannot identify a fixture or outdoor cause, a professional leak-detection assessment can narrow the problem before any concrete or flooring is disturbed.

Safety note: If water is actively spreading, shut off the main water supply if you can do so safely. Keep away from wet electrical equipment and call emergency services if there is an immediate electrical, fire, or structural danger.

What Professional Slab Leak Detection Can Do

A qualified technician starts with the symptoms, plumbing layout, meter observations, and areas where moisture or temperature changes were noticed. Depending on the pipe material and site conditions, the diagnostic process may include pressure testing, acoustic listening, thermal imaging, electronic pipe locating, or another appropriate method. The purpose is to confirm whether a leak is present, narrow its location, and give you better information for the repair decision.

Pressure testing

Helps identify whether an isolated section is holding pressure as expected.

Acoustic listening

Uses sound from escaping water to narrow a suspected location under finished surfaces.

Thermal or electronic tools

May reveal temperature differences or help trace pipe routes when conditions allow.

A diagnosis is not the same as a repair estimate. The best repair option depends on the leak location, pipe condition, access, flooring, and whether the problem appears isolated or part of a wider pipe issue. Possible approaches include a localized repair, rerouting a line, or a larger repiping plan.

Frequently Asked Questions

Can a high water bill prove that I have a slab leak?

No. It can indicate a leak, but it does not identify the location or prove that the leak is beneath the slab. Check other possible causes and request professional testing if the change is unexplained.

How can I check my water meter?

Turn off all water use and watch the meter or its low-flow indicator for several minutes. Movement suggests flow somewhere in the system, but it cannot locate the leak by itself.

What if water is actively spreading across the floor?

If safe, shut off the main water supply, avoid wet electrical areas, document the damage, and contact qualified local help. Call emergency services for an immediate electrical, fire, or structural danger.

What tools may be used for slab leak detection?

Depending on conditions, a professional may use pressure testing, acoustic listening, thermal imaging, electronic pipe locating, or another suitable diagnostic method.
